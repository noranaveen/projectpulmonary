The Forests of the Human Body: How Smoke Impacts Both Lungs & Trees

Analyzing Structural Similarities, Damage Mechanisms, and Mitigation Methods

CD
Celine Donlon
Project Pulmonary Writer · 2 min read
Exposure Science

Introduction

If you were to unroll and connect every single internal airway branch from your lungs end-to-end, they would be able to extend to an estimated 1,500 miles (approximately 2,414 kilometers). This is about hundreds of miles longer than the Amazon Rainforest, from the Atlantic Coast to the Andean Foothills.

Structural Similarities

While the comparison between lungs and forests may seem like a stretch, both possess branching structures that support life. Lungs are the main organ of the respiratory system; they supply the body with oxygen and dispose of carbon dioxide. On the other hand, trees are supplied with carbon dioxide and release oxygen.

Overall, trees and lungs have a similar structure called a branching network, where smoke unfortunately disrupts the flow of both: clogging a lung’s airways and suffocating leaves in the exact same way.

Damage Mechanism

Because lungs are the primary organs that help us breathe, it is essential to prevent harmful smoke particles from entering them. Lungs are compacted with a complex bronchial tree-like structure, branching from large airways and transitioning into smaller bronchioles that end in alveoli, tiny air sacs where oxygen and carbon dioxide are exchanged. From either wildfire smoke or cigarette smoke, particles like PM2.5 (fine particulate matter with a diameter of 2.5 micrometers or smaller) can enter deep into the lungs and pose major health risks such as lung inflammation, or even translocate into the bloodstream and affect other organs.

Similarly, trees help Earth breathe. Through intricate pores on their leaves called stomata, trees absorb carbon dioxide and release oxygen through a process called photosynthesis. However, just as PM2.5 infiltrates the human lungs, wildfires as well as industrial smoke close and clog a trees’ stomata. As a result, the process of photosynthesis can be interrupted, which ultimately reduces oxygen production and puts the tree at risk for premature death.

Mitigation Methods

To mitigate these effects on both our lungs and trees, we can start off by being considerate of the danger of smoke and its impact on both our lungs and Earths’. In other words, we can take prescient measures to protect our pulmonary health and preserve our forests. Individually, we can take safety measures like monitoring air quality and staying indoors during severe weather. As a community, we can avoid carelessly using flames especially during dry or windy weather to prevent the risk of starting wildfires. Taking action to protect both our lungs and trees can make a significant difference for both the environment and human health.

Conclusion

Ultimately, as the purpose of sustaining life applies to both Earth’s trees and our lungs, the danger of smoke also threatens them both. Particles such as PM2.5 can enter both of their systems, and risk major health complications as well as premature death. Nevertheless, we can prevent smoke exposure by taking safety measures for both our lungs and trees.
