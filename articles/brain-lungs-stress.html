The Connection Between the Brain & Lungs: How Stress Can Affect Pulmonary Health

Exploring the Vagus Nerve, Recent Research, and the Role of Adrenaline & Cortisol

CD
Celine Donlon
Project Pulmonary Writer · 2 min read
Health & Science

Introduction

A common misconception in public health is that the only people vulnerable to pulmonary diseases are those who expose their lungs to smoke, namely fire-fighters and smokers. In reality, millions of people are at risk in developing lung diseases from a simple systemic response: psychological stress.

Background & Context

Apart from being a natural emotional response, stress triggers a powerful systemic response that can damage lung function and significantly exacerbate pulmonary diseases.

Since it was first discovered, the relationship between respiratory diseases and psychological traits remained understudied due to scientists dismissing psychological traits as a side effect of a contracted respiratory disease. Despite the contributory relationship being substantially understudied, the concept has started to become popular among modern scientists.

The Role of the Vagus Nerve

Despite the brains and lungs being a part of two different physiological systems (the central nervous system and respiratory system), they are both connected by the vagus nerve. The vagus nerve is the longest cranial nerve in the human body and carries bidirectional signals between the brain and major internal organs, including the lungs. This means that if the brain were to undergo any kind of emotional instability and stress, disruptive signals would instantly be sent from the vagus nerve to the lungs.

Recent Research

Recently, modern scientists have discovered results of what stress signals from the brain specifically do to the lungs. To elaborate, they have found that psychological stress can significantly worsen chronic lung diseases like asthma and COPD (chronic obstructive pulmonary disease) by increasing airway inflammation, triggering bronchospasms (the muscles in the lungs’ airways tightening), and making breathing more difficult.

The Role of Adrenaline & Cortisol

Furthermore, stress triggers hormones such as adrenaline and cortisol. These hormones are the primary chemical messengers that are released by the body during a “fight-or-flight” response. They are both responsible for the rush of energy in the body when reacting to a challenging situation. Despite their short-term benefits in a stressful situation, both of these hormones can physically alter a tissue’s environment and immune responses if the body is constantly exposed to them.

Conclusion

Even if smoke is easy to avoid from checking air quality and eschewing smoking habits, stress is an unavoidable factor that plays a significant role in daily life as it can worsen lung health long-term as well as exacerbate already existing pulmonary health-related problems. Because of this, it is essential to manage stress and be considerate of mental health to protect long-term respiratory health.
