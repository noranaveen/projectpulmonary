Can Stem Cells Reverse Pulmonary Fibrosis?

Evaluating Cellular Repair Mechanisms in End-Stage Lung Disease

SR
Sheethal Raveendra
Enloe Magnet High School · Advisor: Siri Damu · 5 min read
Research Papers

Abstract

Idiopathic pulmonary fibrosis (IPF) carries a median survival of 3 to 5 years after diagnosis, a mortality rate comparable to aggressive cancers. Existing drugs only slow progression without repairing damaged architecture, which has driven interest in stem cell therapies—specifically induced pluripotent stem cells (iPSCs) and mesenchymal stem cells (MSCs)—to actively regenerate tissue. Currently, 90% of active stem cell trials for pulmonary fibrosis remain in early Phase I and II testing. This paper evaluates the clinical potential of stem cells in treating pulmonary fibrosis, examining whether iPSC- and MSC-derived therapies can overcome Phase I/II barriers to actively reverse alveolar scarring rather than just delay fibrotic decline. A systematic search of clinical databases and literature was conducted using key terms including "Pulmonary fibrosis," "stem cell," "Induced Pluripotent Stem Cells," "Mesenchymal Stem Cells/Stromal Cells," and "alveolar tissue regeneration." Findings indicate that while MSCs provide strong immunomodulatory and anti-inflammatory effects that help prevent early damage, iPSCs offer distinct regenerative capacity by directly replacing damaged alveolar epithelial cells and blocking fibrotic signals. However, clinical implementation is limited by the strict timing required for MSC administration, alongside tumorigenic risks and differentiation hurdles for iPSCs. Unregulated direct-to-consumer cell therapies also pose safety risks that demand strict regulatory oversight. Moving stem cells from disease management to true alveolar reversal will require standardized differentiation protocols and non-viral targeting methods.

Introduction

Pulmonary fibrosis occurs when damaged lung tissue becomes thick and scarred, making breathing progressively more difficult. The disease primarily affects middle-aged and older adults, though infants and children are occasionally diagnosed. The resulting lung damage is irreversible. While medications and therapies can slow scarring, manage symptoms, and improve quality of life, some patients ultimately require a lung transplant (Mayo Clinic, 2018). Global clinical trials investigating stem cell therapies for pulmonary fibrosis have grown over past decades, introducing new research directions and treatment approaches. Previous work demonstrates that unlike MSCs, which primarily regulate immune responses and inflammation, iPSCs directly promote tissue regeneration to prevent scarring. However, iPSCs carry potential tumorigenic risks, requiring refined differentiation protocols and non-viral, site-specific targeting to improve safety. Because clinical data on iPSC therapy for pulmonary fibrosis remains limited, additional studies are necessary to confirm safety and efficacy (Shi et al., 2026). This paper evaluates the therapeutic efficacy and safety profiles of iPSCs versus MSCs in reversing alveolar damage, based on a systematic search of clinical trials using the terms "Pulmonary fibrosis," "stem cell," "Induced Pluripotent Stem Cells," "Mesenchymal Stem Cells/Stromal Cells," and "alveolar tissue regeneration." The analysis covers disease background, existing treatment limitations, emerging stem cell therapies, iPSC and MSC characteristics, and associated safety risks and protocols.

Current Treatment Limits

Several limitations in the review process should be noted when evaluating these findings. First, while critical appraisal involved three researchers per paper to maintain objectivity and evaluate study quality, this step relies on expert judgment. A different reviewer team might have reached different conclusions during appraisal, altering the final selection of included studies. Second, manual screening of 1,836 articles across 10 reviewers introduces potential human error and variability in selection habits. Because most papers were eliminated during initial screening prior to detailed appraisal, some relevant studies on lung stem cell therapies may have been excluded (Silverstein et al., 2024).

Mesenchymal Stem Cells

In bleomycin-induced lung injury models, MSCs demonstrate multi-target therapeutic effects, including cell homing, direct differentiation, secretome release, and stimulation of endogenous repair responses. However, most available studies focus on the early inflammatory phase rather than the late fibrotic phase. Consequently, MSCs are predominantly evaluated as a preventive intervention rather than a treatment for established scarring, making administration timing critical.

Despite ongoing clinical debates regarding the safety and overall efficacy of MSCs for IPF, mobilizing endogenous bone marrow-derived MSCs remains an alternative area of study. Additionally, although microRNAs contribute to fibrosis development, their ability to enhance MSC performance has not been fully mapped (Li et al., 2017).

Induced Pluripotent Stem Cells

iPSCs offer distinct regenerative capabilities in pulmonary fibrosis models. Unlike stem cell types that act mainly through anti-inflammatory mechanisms, iPSCs can differentiate directly into functional lung cells, allowing them to repair and rebuild damaged alveolar structures. The principal challenge with iPSCs involves safety, specifically the risk of tumor or teratoma formation if differentiation is incomplete or uncontrolled. Research therefore focuses on developing non-viral targeting methods and improving differentiation precision prior to broad clinical testing. Because clinical trial data on iPSCs for pulmonary disease remains in early stages, further human studies are needed to determine long-term safety, optimal delivery timing, and therapeutic performance relative to other approaches (Tsutsui et al., 2026).

Safety Risks

Despite theoretical support for stem cell applications in lung disease, notable safety concerns persist regarding their short- and long-term biological activity. Given the limited clinical evidence available, administering unproven stem cell treatments introduces risks of adverse events or accelerated disease progression.

Safety concerns are compounded by the proliferation of unregulated direct-to-consumer stem cell clinics. These operations often charge high out-of-pocket costs for unverified treatments that lack peer-reviewed support or oversight from regulatory authorities such as the FDA. Major respiratory organizations, including the American Lung Association, advise against unapproved procedures due to unproven efficacy and safety risks. Legitimate stem cell treatments for pulmonary conditions are regulated as drugs, restricting credible investigation to monitored clinical trials (Rgadmin, 2025).

Conclusion

IPF remains a fatal condition with limited therapeutic options, as current FDA-approved drugs only slow functional decline without restoring scarred tissue. Stem cell approaches shift the focus of IPF therapy from symptom management toward tissue repair.

MSCs and iPSCs present distinct mechanisms of action. MSCs function primarily through immunomodulatory and paracrine signaling, addressing early inflammatory damage, but their capacity to reverse established late-stage scarring is limited by timing constraints. iPSCs demonstrate direct regenerative capacity by repopulating damaged alveolar epithelial tissue and altering fibrotic signaling, though clinical adoption requires resolving tumorigenic risks, differentiation efficiency, and the lack of late-phase clinical trial data. Safety oversight remains necessary to protect patients from unregulated stem cell procedures.

Methodologically, this synthesis was limited by manual literature screening, where reviewer judgment during critical appraisal and screening variability across reviewers may have affected study selection. Advancing stem cell applications for IPF requires standardized differentiation protocols, non-viral delivery methods, and regulated Phase III trials to evaluate whether these treatments can safely reverse alveolar damage in patients.
