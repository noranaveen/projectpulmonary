Is Consciousness Just Biology?

The Brain and the Mystery of Experience

PP
Project Pulmonary
Research Team · 3 min read
Health & Science

Imagine seeing the color red for the first time as a baby; A scientist can explain the wavelengths of light entering your eyes, and check how the brain uses that information and which neurons become active. But would that show why red looks red to you ? Humans in modern times can study almost everything that happens inside the brain, but there is still a gap between knowing how something works and knowing what it feels like to experience it. This gap is what makes consciousness such a difficult problem for science.

What Happens When the Brain Stops Working?

There is strong evidence that consciousness depends on the brain. Anesthesia can make a person lose awareness; and certain brain injuries can change how someone experiences the world mentally. Consciousness is not controlled by just one small part of the brain, but rather it involves cooperation between different regions in the brain and neural pathways that help us process information and be “aware” (Paris Brain Institute, n.d.).

These evidently show that consciousness is biological. Changing the brain changes someone's experience. But there is a distinct difference between finding the physical processes behind an experience and explaining the experience itself mentally. Scientists might identify the brain activity associated with seeing red. Why that activity comes with a “subjective” feeling is a complex question to resolve. Are scientists really explaining consciousness, or just describing what the brain does when we are conscious? For example: How a computer is working vs. describing how the tasks are being done by the computer while it’s working.

Two Theories, One Unanswered Question

Scientists have developed different theories to explain consciousness from brain activity. Global Neuronal Workspace Theory suggests that information becomes conscious when it is shared across a wider neural network of the brain. Integrated Information Theory focuses on how information is combined within a system in the brain. Both are trying to explain why some information enters our awareness while other information does not.

In 2025, researchers tested these two theories using brain data from 256 participants. Their findings supported some aspects of the theories but challenged important parts of both theories (Ferrante et al., 2025). This shows that neither theory can simply be treated as the final answer. Even with modern advancements such as advanced brain scans and large amounts of data, scientists are still confuzzled on which explanations hold up.

Is There More to Experience Than Biology?

Philosopher Thomas Nagel explored this problem in animals in his 1974 essay, “What Is It Like to Be a Bat?” A scientist could learn about a bat's brain, senses, and behavior without knowing what it is actually like to experience the world as a bat. For example, we can understand the biological process of echolocation, without understanding how the bat experiences its surroundings using the echolocation.

Nagel's argument does not entirely prove that consciousness exists outside the physical brain. But, it does raise a question about the limits of a specific explanation; Even if we eventually understand every biological process used in consciousness, would that tell us what those processes feel like from the inside?

For now, neither possibility has been finalized.

So, Is Consciousness Just Biology?

The evidence strongly connects consciousness to the brain, and ignoring that evidence due to theoretical works is ignorance. However, saying that the brain produces or supports consciousness is still not the same as explaining consciousness completely.

It could be that subjective experience is what certain biological processes feel like from the inside. If this was the case, understanding the brain would most likely give us the answer. The problem is that we don’t know how to properly connect the physical processes we can measure, with the distinct experiences we can’t quite yet “measure” (since it's subjective).

That ends us up with a question science hasn’t answered (yet): if we could map every neuron, neural network and biological processes involved in seeing red, would we finally understand the experience of seeing it as if we actually did see it, or would we still be missing something?
