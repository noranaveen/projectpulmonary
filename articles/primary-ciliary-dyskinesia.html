Primary Ciliary Dyskinesia in the Lungs: How the Dysfunction of Cilia Affects Pulmonary Health

Looking at PCD’s Genetic Background, Effects, & Treatments

CD
Celine Donlon
Project Pulmonary Writer · 2 min read
Lung Diseases

Introduction

Primary ciliary dyskinesia (PCD) is a rare hereditary disorder that causes the dysfunction of cilia, microscopic hair-like membrane structures found on the surface of most Eukaryotic structures. For our lungs, the purpose of these structures is to maintain clear airways.

The impairment of these tiny organelles mean that they may be:

  • Missing
  • Deformed
  • Immobile
  • Moving abnormally

These factors prevent cilia from being able to functionally remove foreign particles from different organs in the body. For cilia in the lungs, this means that particles like mucus remain in our lungs, which can lead to serious health problems such as chronic coughing. Over time, this debris greatly increases vulnerability to serious respiratory issues. This includes conditions such as bronchitis, pneumonia, asthma, or chronic obstructive pulmonary disease (COPD).

Genetic Background of PCD

PCD is a hereditary disease, meaning it can only come from inheriting it from a family member. It is primarily a recessive gene where both parents both have to be heterozygous carriers for the child to inherit it. Furthermore, it is autosomal, meaning biological sex does not play a role in whether or not it is developed in a child. Statistically, PCD carries a 25% risk of inheritance.

Long-Term Effects of PCD

Despite simply affecting the surface of the body’s cells, Primary ciliary dyskinesia actually poses many challenges to its host’s lungs. Because it is unable to clean mucus, bacteria, and other particles from the lungs, these particles can build up in the lungs and cause long-term problems. Due to build up, people with PCD can contract serious pulmonary diseases like bronchiectasis or develop infections. Gradually, this can cause progressive tissue damage and significantly lower lung capacity. Staying consistent with these treatments is essential for maintaining long-term pulmonary health.

Treatments

Primary ciliary dyskinesia is not curable like most genetic conditions. However, it is treatable from methods like regular chest physiotherapy and antibiotics. Physiotherapy helps clear up mucus in the lungs, improving their functions and reducing the chance of developing infections in the lungs. On the other hand, antibiotics are taken to combat infections. Due to PCD being chronic, antibiotics are commonly taken by patients long-term for life.

While primary ciliary dyskinesia is currently incurable from its complexity, vital relief is provided from treatments like antibiotics and consistent chest physiotherapy.
